# Artificial Intelligence at What Cost?

**Author:** Arthur Leroy Jones — ORCID 0009-0007-2985-5132 — arthurjones@trialityresearch.org
**Compiled:** October 26, 2026
**Version:** 1.0
**Scope:** Public sources only (company disclosures, IDC/UBS/Counterpoint/TrendForce estimates, trade reporting). Shares differ by definition — shipments, revenue, installed locations, and capacity are not the same thing; see §10.
**Note:** Companion to the U.S. resource-use figures document.

**Abstract.** This paper inventories the full cost of artificial intelligence, from the minerals in a single rack to the thermodynamics underneath the entire buildout. It begins with the supply chain as it stands — accelerator concentration, the three-company memory oligopoly, foundry and packaging chokepoints, the ODM assembly layer, and the materials (copper, aluminum, cobalt, lithium, nickel, graphite, rare earths) that go into the machines — then follows the constraints that decide whether that chain can keep scaling: transistor physics, the AC-to-DC conversion tax, water treatment, the 2026–2028 balance between surplus raw tonnage and sold-out finished inputs, the human and environmental cost of mining, climate and diesel, and heat reuse. It continues through the software moat, the decommissioning wave, and the rest of AI: models, data, the people who label it, the financing, the three-bloc governance split, safety, history, recursive AI, and children whose critical-thinking steps are skipped when the work is offloaded. It closes with the principle underneath all of it: efficiency does not eliminate cost, it moves it — from chip to cooling tower to river to atmosphere — and the bill, diffused and delayed, always arrives. All figures are drawn from public sources.

## 1. Anatomy of a rack — what actually goes into one

The modern AI rack is no longer a box of servers. It is a rack-scale system, sold and cooled as a unit. The reference design is NVIDIA's GB200 NVL72:

| Component | GB200 NVL72 (per rack) |
| --- | --- |
| GPUs | 72 × B200 Blackwell (1,000 W each) |
| CPUs | 36 × Grace (72 cores each), one per GB200 Superchip (1 CPU + 2 GPUs) |
| GPU memory | 13.4 TB HBM3e (192 GB per GPU) |
| CPU memory | Up to 17 TB LPDDR5X |
| Interconnect inside the rack | 9 × NVSwitch boards, NVLink 5.0, 130 TB/s aggregate bandwidth — all 72 GPUs act as one accelerator |
| Network out of the rack | 8 × 400G InfiniBand (Quantum-2) or Spectrum-X Ethernet; ConnectX-7 or BlueField-3 SuperNICs; up to 200G in-band management via BlueField-3 DPUs |
| Storage bays | 144 × E1.S PCIe 5.0 drive bays |
| Power | About 120–132 kW per rack |
| Cooling | Direct liquid cooling, mandatory; ~20 L/min coolant flow at sub-30°C inlet |
| Physical | 48U, 19-inch, 600 × 1,068 × 2,236 mm, about 1.36 metric tons |
| Price | Roughly $2–3 million per rack |

The previous generation for comparison: an HGX H100 node holds 8 GPUs and 2 CPUs; the older DGX POD comparison puts 64 H100s across 8 racks at about 320 kW total, against one NVL72 rack at about 120 kW.

The next step, already ramping: **GB300 NVL72 ("Blackwell Ultra")** — 72 × B300 at 1,400 W each, 288 GB HBM3e per GPU, ConnectX-8 800G networking, rack power about 132–142 kW, still direct liquid cooling. Analysts project up to roughly 60,000 Blackwell Ultra racks shipping in 2026, driven by Microsoft, Amazon, and Meta. Beyond it, the Vera Rubin NVL144 generation (Vera CPUs, HBM4) is expected in the second half of 2026.

Board complexity is part of the story: baseboard layer counts rose from 16–20 layers (H100/H200) to 24–32 (B200) to 32–40+ layers for the NVL72 NVSwitch boards, driven by NVLink 5.0 routing, PCIe Gen6, and higher-current power planes. The NVSwitch boards are among the most complex PCBs manufactured commercially.

**Motherboards, in plain terms:** a rack-scale system contains not one motherboard but a hierarchy of boards — GPU baseboards (SXM modules), CPU/GPU Superchip modules, NVSwitch boards, network adapter cards, and power distribution boards — all of which must be designed, fabricated, and assembled before a single rack ships. That board stack is where much of the supply chain concentrates.

## 3. The custom-silicon insurgency — hyperscalers designing their own chips

Every major hyperscaler now runs a production custom-accelerator program. The motive is the same everywhere: NVIDIA's margins (70%+ gross) and permanent shortage turn dependence into a ballooning bill, and a chip built for one well-understood workload beats a general-purpose GPU on cost and power at hyperscale.

| Amazon (AWS) | Trainium2 / Trainium3; Inferentia; Graviton CPUs; Nitro offload | Trainium2 "Project Rainier" supercluster ~500K+ chips; Trainium3 UltraServers generally available | Marvell + Annapurna Labs (in-house) | Anthropic Claude training, Bedrock |
| Meta | MTIA (v2 deployed; 300–500 series rolling out) | Multi-gigawatt roadmap with Broadcom; 1+ GW initial phase through 2029; separate 6 GW AMD deal (Feb, reportedly up to $60B) | Broadcom | Ranking, ads, Llama inference |

Structural point: **Broadcom touches three of the five frontier custom programs** (Meta, Google, OpenAI); Marvell anchors the fourth (Amazon); Microsoft is the holdout leaning on internal design. That concentration of design IP inside Broadcom is why Wall Street prices a $100B+ AI revenue outlook for it. Google is the most advanced — its TPU program is nearly a decade old and Gemini runs almost entirely on Google-designed hardware.

## 4. Memory — the three-company oligopoly under every accelerator

High-bandwidth memory (HBM) is 3D-stacked DRAM that delivers 5–10× the bandwidth of standard GDDR. AI accelerators are memory-bandwidth-bound: if HBM cannot feed the processors fast enough, the expensive GPUs sit idle. Every serious AI accelerator depends on it, and only three companies make it.

| Supplier | HBM revenue share, Q2 2026 (Counterpoint) | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| SK Hynix (South Korea) | 50% (58% in Q1) | First to mass-produce HBM3E; majority of NVIDIA supply; ~54% of NVIDIA's HBM in 2026 (J.P. Morgan estimate) |
| Samsung (South Korea) | 33% (21% in Q1) | Ramping 12-high HBM3E; yields improved from 65% to 78% by Q1 2026 |
| Micron (United States) | 18% (21% in Q1) | Supplies select designs; gaining in inference-optimized HBM |

Other figures on the same market:

- **South Korea produces over 85% of global HBM** (SK Hynix + Samsung).
- **NVIDIA consumes an estimated 65–70% of global HBM production**, and its $500B agreement with SK Hynix secures first claim on that output — a projected **37% of all 2027 HBM capacity**. SK Hynix expects memory demand to exceed supply through at least 2030.
- HBM3E pricing: about **$300 per 36 GB 12-high stack** (~$8.33/GB factory-gate), a 5–6× premium over equivalent DDR5, reflecting 3D stacking and through-silicon vias. HBM4 is estimated near $550 per 36 GB stack.
- Capacity: industry-wide HBM capacity in 2025 was about **1.2 million 12-high stack equivalents**, with a 15–20% shortage driving allocation to major customers and ~25% HBM3E price increases in Q1 2026.
- Per-GPU appetite: NVIDIA shipped an estimated 3.2 million H100-equivalent GPUs in 2025, each with 6 HBM stacks, consuming about 19.2 million HBM stacks; AMD added about 0.8 million units.
- A January 2026 analysis of a 32,000-GPU cluster found HBM3 bandwidth was the limiting factor for 37% of training steps.

## 5. The fab layer — two chokepoints under everything

**Lithography (Netherlands).** ASML of Veldhoven is the **sole commercial supplier of EUV lithography**, the technology required to print the finest features on leading-edge chips — effectively 100% of the leading edge, and the only shipper of High-NA EUV. Each machine is the size of a bus, weighs about 180 tons, costs roughly €200 million (up to ~$400 million for the most advanced), contains over 100,000 components from ~5,000 suppliers (Zeiss optics, Cymer light source, Trumpf lasers), takes about a year to build, and ships in ~40 freight containers or three 747s. ASML makes only about 50 a year. Its order book was €38.8 billion at end-2025; FY2025 revenue over $37 billion; market value roughly $677–692 billion in 2026. No competitor — Nikon, Canon, or any Chinese entrant — has a viable EUV system, and the Netherlands has blocked export of the most advanced machines to China since 2019.

**Fabrication and packaging (Taiwan, expanding to the U.S.).** TSMC fabricates essentially all leading AI accelerators and performs the advanced packaging that turns dies into finished chips:

- **CoWoS (Chip-on-Wafer-on-Substrate)** bonds logic dies and HBM stacks to a silicon interposer with through-silicon vias, delivering the multi-terabyte-per-second bandwidth AI chips require. No organic substrate can replicate that density, and there is no merchant market for CoWoS modules — it runs captive at TSMC.
- Capacity: about **130,000 wafers/month by end-2026, planned to roughly double to ~260,000 by end-2028**, centered on the AP7 facility in Taiwan and the Arizona campus. TSMC has roughly doubled CoWoS capacity annually since 2023 (from ~35,000/month in late 2024), and demand has still outrun it.
- The bottleneck is binding: TSMC's CEO has said CoWoS is **sold out through 2026**, with assembly lead times of 52–78 weeks. Google reportedly cut its 2026 TPU production target by about 25% (from ~4 million to ~3 million units) on CoWoS constraints.
- Geography: TSMC currently sends **100% of chips to Taiwan for advanced packaging — including chips fabricated at its Phoenix, Arizona plant** (a ~12,000-mile round trip). It operates five advanced packaging facilities in Taiwan, and is investing $165 billion in Arizona (up to 12 facilities, including two packaging fabs; AP1 mass production targeted 2028). For the first time, the U.S. would have end-to-end advanced semiconductor manufacturing on its own soil.
- Alternatives: Intel's EMIB-T is estimated at 15,000–20,000 CoWoS-equivalent wafers/month in 2027 rising to 40,000–45,000 in 2028, potentially suited to Google and Amazon custom accelerators; UMC and Amkor absorb spillover demand.
- TSMC's 2026 capital spending is $60–64 billion, 70–80% to advanced processes; a 5.5-reticle CoWoS platform in mass production in 2026 reports yields above 98%.

The Taiwanese ODM core: **Foxconn (Hon Hai)** — about 40% global AI server share, with AI server rack shipments that could double in 2026; Q1 revenue NT$2.13 trillion ($66.6B, +29.7% year over year); investing $136 million to expand AI server production in Mexico. **Quanta / QCT** — Q1 revenue NT$809.2 billion ($25.3B, +66.6%). **Wistron** — Q1 revenue NT$846.3 billion ($26.5B, +144.3%), a primary assembler of next-generation NVIDIA rack systems. **Wiwynn** — from 12th to 5th largest in Taiwan's 88-company AI supply chain list since 2022. **Inventec, Pegatron, Compal** round out the group. The money split is the telling part (AmCham Taiwan analysis): **Taiwan's chipmakers earn 56% of the supply chain's gross profit on 20% of its revenue, while server assemblers take 69% of revenue but keep only 23% of gross profit** — assemblers pass through the cost of every component they buy, which inflates revenue relative to profit. Since early 2022, Foxconn has added about NT$5 trillion to annual revenue, Quanta's revenue is up nearly 290%, and Wistron's is up more than 380%.

Geographic shift: in response to tariffs, **seven major Taiwanese ODMs are expanding U.S. production, particularly in Texas** — Foxconn ($33M Houston land acquisition), Quanta and Wistron (Texas and California), Pegatron, Inventec, Compal, and Wiwynn evaluating U.S. sites — with individual server assembly plants requiring $3–5 billion investments. White-box (ODM-built, OCP-spec) servers were a $22.48B market in 2025, projected to $82.61B by 2035 (13.9% CAGR), as hyperscalers cut per-unit costs 20–30% versus branded gear. North America holds 32.4% of hyperscaler capex; Asia-Pacific is the fastest-growing region at 16.3% CAGR.

## 7. Networking, power, and cooling — the systems around the silicon

A rack is limited as much by what can be fed into it (power) and drawn out of it (cooling) as by the processors themselves.

**Networking.** NVIDIA's ConnectX SuperNICs and BlueField DPUs carry traffic out of the rack over InfiniBand (Quantum-2, 400G) or Spectrum-X Ethernet; GB300 moves to ConnectX-8 800G, with 1.6T OSFP links becoming standard for the Rubin generation. Broadcom's Tomahawk switch silicon and its custom-ASIC design partnerships (see §3) make it the second networking center of gravity. Accton (Taiwan) makes the switches connecting servers to one another and has climbed from 28th to 12th in Taiwan's AI supply chain ranking since 2022.

**Power.** Delta Electronics packages 800-volt DC distribution (up to 98% conversion efficiency facility- and rack-level; 98.5% at board level) with liquid cooling into a single stack for NVIDIA-based AI factories, including in-row equipment delivering up to 800 kW from a single power rack. The shift to 800 VDC reduces conversion stages, losses, and the copper needed to feed power-dense racks.

**Cooling.** The liquid cooling market is scaling with rack power: DC Market Insights estimates the market at **$8.84 billion in 2026, reaching $27.40 billion by 2035** (16.79% CAGR base case), with 97% of new AI capacity liquid-cooled by 2035. The top three suppliers — Boyd Thermal (acquired by Eaton, March 2026, for $9.55B; ~17% share), Vertiv, and CoolIT Systems (acquired by Ecolab, July 2026, for $4.75B; ~6.2%) — together hold about 38% of the market. The coolant distribution unit (CDU) segment alone is projected from **$1.05 billion (2025) to $7.74 billion (2032), a 33.0% CAGR**, led by Schneider Electric, Vertiv, Delta, and nVent. Delta lists liquid-to-liquid CDUs at 2.4 MW and 3 MW. What that cooling water actually demands chemically — treatment, Legionella control, blowdown — is covered in §18.

## 8. Materials — copper is the mass, rare earths are the pinch

From the project's own atomic-compute white paper, the material base of a data center includes structural steel, reinforced concrete, high-purity copper (transmission, grounding grids, bus bars, network), ultra-pure silica fiber, silicon and rare earths (neodymium, dysprosium, cobalt in GPUs/SSDs), aluminum (racks, trays, heat exchangers), synthetic coolants and dielectric fluids, and LFP or VRLA battery arrays for UPS.

Copper, quantified (S&P Global, *Copper in the Age of AI*, January 2026):

- Data-center-linked copper demand: **1.1 million tonnes in 2025 → 2.5 million tonnes by 2040**; AI training facilities expected to account for 58% of data center copper demand by 2030.
- A standard facility uses **5,000–15,000 tonnes** of copper; a hyperscale AI training site as much as **50,000 tonnes**; a 100 MW hyperscale data center on the order of **4,700 tonnes** for wiring and cooling.
- CRU (Summer 2026 outlook): AI data centers will consume **682,000 tonnes of copper**, up 138% from its 2024 forecast. Wood Mackenzie: AI electricity requirements alone drive grid copper demand to 1.1 million tonnes a year by 2030.
- One August 2026 study found **power infrastructure, not semiconductors, is the main driver of mineral demand from AI data centers, with copper accounting for 83% of total modelled mineral mass**.
- Total global copper demand: 28 million tonnes (2025) → 42 million tonnes (2040); without major new mining investment, a supply gap near 10 million tonnes. A new copper mine averages **17 years** from discovery to production. Refined production grew only 0.9% in 2026, with a 150,000-tonne deficit already recorded. For where these materials come from geopolitically, see §17; for whether supply meets demand in 2026–2028, see §19.

## 10. Chokepoints — the single points of failure

The whole stack narrows to a handful of gates, each with no ready substitute:

1. **ASML EUV (Netherlands)** — one company, ~50 machines a year, every leading chip depends on it.
2. **TSMC CoWoS (Taiwan)** — sold out through 2026; every NVIDIA, AMD, Google, Amazon, and Microsoft accelerator passes through it; 100% of even Arizona-fabricated chips currently route to Taiwan for this step.
3. **HBM (South Korea)** — three suppliers, one holding half the market; NVIDIA alone claims over a third of next year's capacity.
4. **Taiwan rack assembly** — about 40% of AI servers from one company (Foxconn), half of server revenue from ODM Direct; the U.S. Texas expansion is a response to tariffs, not yet a replacement.
5. **CUDA and the software stack (United States)** — the least physical chokepoint and the hardest to route around, since nearly all large-scale AI infrastructure is built on it.

## 11. Caveats — read before reusing any number

- **Shares depend on the definition.** NVIDIA at ~92% (IDC accelerators), ~71% (UBS locations), ~81% (revenue), and 98% (2023 unit shipments) are all "correct" under different measures. Do not mix them.
- **Analyst estimates vary.** HBM shares (Counterpoint vs Bernstein), market sizes (liquid cooling, white-box servers), and shipment projections come from different firms with different methods; ranges are given where sources disagree.
- **Dates matter.** Most figures here are H1–Q3 2026 vintages; the GB300 ramp, Arizona packaging fabs, and the Texas ODM expansion are all moving targets.
- **Company disclosures are partial.** Hyperscaler chip deployment figures are often "committed," "expected," or "up to" numbers, not audited installed counts.

## 12. Outlook — the fourth memory supplier? CXMT and China's HBM3

The three-company HBM oligopoly in §4 has a first challenger: **ChangXin Memory Technologies (CXMT)**, China's largest DRAM maker, based in Hefei. It is not a new company (founded 2016), but it is newly entering HBM — and it is doing so at **HBM3**, the generation that feeds NVIDIA's H100 and H200, rather than the HBM3E/HBM4 the incumbents ship now.

| CXMT position | Detail |
| --- | --- |
| Product | HBM3, 16nm process, 8-high stacks; samples shipped to clients including Huawei in H2 2025 |
| Mass production target | HBM3 by end of 2026; HBM3E aimed at 2027; HBM3E risk production reported started |
| Yields | About 25% reported on 8-high HBM3 stacks (mature lines run far higher) |
| HBM scale | Of ~265,000 wafer starts/month at end-2025, only ~5,000 went to HBM; projected 30,000 by end-2026, 55,000 by end-2027 (global HBM capacity was ~340,000 wafer starts/month at end-2025) |
| Structural handicap | No EUV access under export controls — needs roughly 30% more wafers than competitors for equivalent output; its DDR5 die runs about 40% larger than Samsung's equivalent |
| Scale and funding | ~$4.2 billion Shanghai STAR Market IPO; Q1 2026 revenue 50.8 billion yuan, up more than 7× year over year; DRAM share 8% at debut, about 10% in Q2 2026; first company to mass-produce LPDDR6 |
| Customers | Domestic Chinese anchor customers (Huawei and others) — it does not need to beat SK Hynix globally to matter, only to feed China's own AI chips while the big three are sold out |

Two honest readings coexist. One supply-chain roundup flags CXMT as a potential fourth global HBM supplier. Another analyst view holds there is still "no fourth source on a timeline that matters to this cycle." Both can be true: CXMT is real, shipping, and growing — and still one to two generations behind, at low yields and small HBM volume. The three-company monopoly holds for now; CXMT is the first credible crack in it.

None of the accelerator designers owns a factory. NVIDIA, AMD, Broadcom, Apple, Google, Amazon, Microsoft, Meta, and OpenAI all rely on foundries — and the foundry market is more concentrated than any layer above it.

| Samsung Foundry (South Korea) | ~6–7% | ~$3.3B |

The top 10 foundries together posted a record $53.49 billion in Q2 2026, up 29% year over year on AI orders. TSMC has held 73% for two consecutive quarters.

**The leading edge is a three-player race — a "one-and-a-half-supplier market":**

- **TSMC N2 (2nm)** — in volume production since Q4 2025, guiding to 100,000 wafers/month by year-end and 200,000 by 2027, with yields reported at 60–70%+. A 2nm wafer runs about $30,000, roughly double 3nm, and customers pay it because there is no alternative. In Q3 2026, 3nm was 33% of TSMC wafer revenue, 2nm already 8%, everything 7nm and below 79%, and high-performance computing (AI accelerators) 68% of revenue. AMD's MI450 entered production at TSMC on 2nm with CoWoS packaging in Q3 2026, with higher-volume shipments expected in 2027; NVIDIA's Vera Rubin platform is ramping into full production. TSMC's North America head reports at least 10 calls a day about wafer capacity.
- **Samsung SF2 (2nm)** — in mass production since late 2025 (Exynos 2600), yields crossing 50–70% in early 2026, the first time in three nodes Samsung cleared the bar before TSMC's process matured. Tesla's $16.5B AI chip deal anchors its order book, and prototype 2nm production for Tesla's AI5 chip began in Taylor, Texas in September 2026.
- **Intel 18A (2nm-class)** — making Panther Lake tiles at Fab 52 in Chandler, Arizona since October 2025, but at 20–25% good-die yield, which Intel itself calls inadequate; output is capped until yields reach standard levels, expected early 2027. Intel does not rank in the pure-play top 10. Its brighter spot is packaging: its EMIB-T technology was adopted for Google's next-generation TPU.

Japan's Rapidus aims to become the fourth 2nm-class player in H2 2027, with 17 design partners — about 18 months behind the incumbents' first volume milestones.

**The China thread, updated to late 2026.** SMIC is #3 and growing fast (record revenue, 36% year over year) on local AI chip demand — but the "one generation behind" shorthand understates it. Volume production is at 7nm (N+2) via DUV multi-patterning — 34 lithography steps where EUV needs 9 — with the 5nm-class N+3 in pilot and mass production unlikely before late 2026. Against TSMC's 2nm in volume since Q4 2025, that is roughly two and a half nodes, or three to four years. The deeper tax is yield: Ascend 910C yields are reported around 20–40%, against TSMC's 90%+, so most of what SMIC starts gets scrapped and the state subsidizes the difference; SMIC's fabs run above 93% utilization, with an estimated 15–20% of advanced-node output dedicated to Ascend. A domestic EUV prototype was reportedly built in Shenzhen, but experts do not expect working chips from it before 2028–2030. None of this stops the stack from being built — it makes it a volume-over-performance strategy, subsidized, one that CXMT's HBM3 push (§12) is part of, since memory, not logic wafers, is what rations Huawei's output.

## 14. Outlook — the scaling wall: why transistors changed shape instead of shrinking

Silicon scaling is running into physics, not just engineering. As transistors shrink, electrons leak across barriers inside each transistor that are now too thin to hold them. Three leaks arrived in order:

1. **Through the gate.** The insulating oxide under the gate thinned to about a nanometer — a few atoms — and electrons began tunneling straight through it. The industry answered by swapping silicon dioxide for hafnium-based high-k dielectrics at 45nm: a thicker insulator with the same control.
2. **Under the gate, source to drain.** As the channel shortened, the gate lost its grip and the drain's electric field reached through to the source, so current leaked even with the transistor "off" — the short-channel effect.
3. **Straight through the channel.** At a few nanometers, electrons quantum-tunnel directly from source to drain through the energy barrier itself, gate off or not. This one is fundamental, not a defect: it is the physics of the barrier.

That is why FET designs changed shape instead of just shrinking:

**Planar → FinFET → gate-all-around.** The planar transistor had the gate on top only. FinFET (2011) wrapped it around three sides of a fin. Gate-all-around — TSMC's N2, Samsung's SF2, Intel's RibbonFET — wraps the gate around all four sides of stacked nanosheets, so the gate controls the channel completely and leakage drops at the same size. The game became *control*, not *size*.

What comes next follows the same logic: forksheets pack n and p devices closer with a dielectric wall between them; CFET stacks one on top of the other to halve the footprint; channels made of 2D materials like molybdenum disulfide — a channel literally one atom thick, which the gate can control even at sub-nanometer lengths. And there is an inversion: tunnel FETs, which stop fighting tunneling and use it as the switching mechanism itself. Backside power delivery (Intel's PowerVia, TSMC's Super PowerRail with the A16 node) is a separate advance — it moves power connections to the rear of the wafer to cut voltage droop and packing constraints, not a leakage fix.

One footnote: the "2nm" and "1nm" labels are marketing. Nobody builds a 2-nanometer feature — gate pitches remain in the 40-nanometer range. The labels track density generations, not physical size. When people say silicon is at the wall, they mean the *silicon channel* is near its tunneling limit, and the road forward is new shapes and new materials, not smaller silicon.

## 15. Outlook — quantum and hybrid systems: the successor is probably not one thing

The pattern of the industry's history is that when one technology hits a wall, something takes over the growth curve — vacuum tubes to transistors to planar to FinFET. Quantum computing is the most discussed candidate, but its power is narrower than the popular version:

- **Exponentially faster, genuinely:** factoring large numbers (Shor's algorithm, the one that threatens RSA encryption) and simulating quantum systems — molecules and materials, where a classical machine's cost grows exponentially with molecule size and a quantum machine's does not. This is probably the most important real application.
- **Faster, but only quadratically:** searching unsorted data (Grover's algorithm). Meaningful, not magic.
- **No speedup at all:** almost everything a data center actually does — running models, serving requests, databases, numerical crunching. Classical machines stay far better there.

So the honest picture is not quantum *replacing* silicon. It is quantum sitting next to it, as a specialized accelerator for the problems it is built for, with classical machines doing everything else and controlling the quantum machine itself. A quantum computer today is a small quantum chip wrapped in a large classical machine — dilution refrigerators, microwave electronics, and error-correction overhead on the order of a thousand physical qubits per reliable logical qubit.

And that hybrid shape is already how the field works, not a prediction:

- The most successful quantum algorithms running today are hybrid by design. Variational algorithms (VQE for chemistry, QAOA for optimization) run as a loop: the quantum chip evaluates something hard, a classical computer adjusts the parameters, and they iterate hundreds of times. Neither half works without the other.
- The hardware is going the same way. IBM's pitch is "quantum-centric supercomputing" — QPUs bolted onto classical HPC clusters. NVIDIA's CUDA-Q programs exactly that split; AWS Braket runs hybrid jobs; supercomputing centers in Europe, Japan, and the U.S. are installing quantum processors next to classical clusters; NVIDIA's DGX Quantum system with Quantinuum is a classical supercomputer node wired to a trapped-ion QPU.

Quantum has its own wall, too: decoherence — qubits lose their state almost immediately when disturbed — which is why error correction eats so much overhead, and why machines capable of running Shor on real key sizes remain years out.

The complete outlook, then: silicon's wall is real, but the successor is probably a mix — quantum for chemistry and cryptography, photonics for interconnects and inference, 2D materials keeping classical scaling alive a while longer — each taking the piece it is actually good at, with classical systems doing the bulk and the boundary between them becoming where most of the engineering goes.

## 16. Outlook — the conversion tax: an AC grid feeding DC machines

Everything in a rack runs on DC, and the grid delivers AC, so power is converted back and forth several times before a single chip does any work. The chain in a conventional facility — grid AC → UPS → distribution → server power supply → on-board voltage regulators → chip — takes a cut at every step:

- **The UPS** in most facilities is double-conversion: AC in, converted to DC to charge the batteries, inverted back to AC out — roughly 94–96% efficient, so 4–6% is lost just to have backup power sitting there.
- **The server power supply** converts AC to 12V DC at around 90–96% efficiency depending on load; the best titanium-rated units reach ~96% at half load, but less at low load.
- **The on-board voltage regulators** step 12V down to the ~1V chips actually consume, at roughly 85–90%.

Stacked up, perhaps 75–85% of what comes off the grid actually reaches the silicon. The rest becomes heat — and the tax compounds, because that waste heat must then be removed by cooling, which costs power a second time.

The sharper point is about generation. Batteries are DC. Solar panels make DC. Fuel cells make DC. In a conventional setup, solar DC is inverted to AC for the grid, converted back to DC by the UPS, and converted again by the server supply — a DC-to-AC-to-DC round trip that exists purely because the grid speaks AC. A battery-backed DC bus skips conversions entirely. That is why the industry is moving toward 800-volt DC distribution straight into the rack (Delta's architecture claims up to 98% conversion efficiency at facility and rack level) and why the Open Compute Project moved racks from 12V to 48V distribution years ago: higher-voltage DC means less current for the same power, hence less copper and less resistive loss.

AC persists for unglamorous historical reasons: a century of grid built on transformers, which only work with AC, and the fact that DC is harder to switch off — AC current crosses zero 120 times a second, naturally extinguishing arcs in breakers, while a DC arc keeps burning. So protection gear for high-power DC is harder and more expensive. AC won the 1890s "war of the currents" because transformers were the only practical way to change voltage levels — Edison's DC could not be stepped up, which would have required a power plant every mile or two. But the world that decision was rational for no longer exists: generation and loads are both increasingly DC-native, and modern power electronics (IGBTs, silicon carbide) can convert DC voltages efficiently — the missing piece from Edison's day. Even long-distance transmission is switching sides: HVDC lines lose less power over very long distances than AC (no reactive power, no skin effect) and can connect grids that are not synchronized.

During the changeover, the tax is a *transition* tax: the century of AC infrastructure must still be maintained, plus DC conversion layers bolted on top. What forces the transition now is scale. When a rack was 10 kW, a 5% conversion loss was a rounding error. When a single campus is a gigawatt, that same 5% is 50 megawatts — a whole power plant's output — turned into heat that must then be cooled. At that size the tax stops being an inefficiency and becomes a constraint on whether the facility can exist at all.

## 17. Outlook — the ingredients: copper, gold, silver, cobalt, and the rest of the table

A single smartphone uses more than 30 elements. A rack multiplies that by thousands. The ingredient list behind AI hardware:

| Ingredient | Where it goes | Where it pinches |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Copper | Wiring, busbars, motors, PCBs | 83% of the modelled mineral mass in AI data centers; data center demand 1.1 Mt (2025) → 2.5 Mt (2040); Chile holds ~23% of global supply |
| Aluminum | Racks, trays, heat exchangers, heatsinks, enclosures | Guinea mines ~40% of world bauxite and ~70% of seaborne exports; China smelts ~60% of world aluminum; smelting takes 13–15 MWh per tonne — the most energy-intensive common industrial process; recycling takes ~5% of primary energy |
| Gold | Connectors, bonding wire, PCB contacts | Tiny amounts per device, but in essentially everything — the one contact metal that does not corrode, with no equal substitute |
| Silver | Solder, contacts, solar panels, EV power electronics | "The missing critical mineral": absent from official U.S./EU/IEA lists, yet industrial demand has outpaced most listed minerals over five years, with flat mine supply and persistent deficits |
| Cobalt | Battery cathodes (NMC), magnets | DRC mines ~70–74%; China refines ~65%; ~80% of DRC production under Chinese ownership; LFP chemistry avoids cobalt, but total demand still rises on EV volume |
| Lithium | Batteries | Australia, Chile, and China mine over 70%; China refines ~58–65%; EVs were 85% of demand in 2023; IEA net-zero scenarios put demand up 40-fold by 2040 |
| Nickel | Batteries, stainless steel | Indonesia mines over half the world's nickel and holds ~43% of refining capacity |
| Graphite | Battery anodes | China produces ~78% of natural graphite and dominates processing even more |
| Rare earths (neodymium, praseodymium, dysprosium, terbium) | Magnets in motors, speakers, fans, drives | ~90% of production with three nations; ~95% of new EV motors still use rare earth magnets |
| Others | Tantalum (capacitors), tin (solder), gallium, germanium, indium (displays, semiconductors), palladium, platinum (contacts), silicon itself | Small doses, no device complete without them |

The point most often missed: **the mine is no longer the chokepoint — the refinery is.** The DRC digs cobalt but China refines it; Australia and Chile dig lithium but China refines it. Across key energy minerals, China controls about 72% of global *processing* capacity (IEA 2026). Export restrictions on critical minerals have risen roughly 5× since 2009 (OECD). That is also why recycling has become strategic: companies now recover copper, gold, silver, and aluminum from old equipment and lithium, nickel, and cobalt from dead batteries, because the scrap pile is one of the few sources no single country controls. The quantities in the machines themselves are in §8; the 2026–2028 balance is in §19.

The scale of what is coming: critical mineral demand overall could grow 3.5× by 2030, needing an estimated $500 billion in mining investment by 2040 — for mines that take 10–17 years to open.

## 18. Outlook — the water treatment behind the water numbers

Behind every "gallons per day" figure in §8 sits a treatment plant. A modern AI facility runs two different water systems, each with its own purification burden.

**The cooling-tower loop (open loop)** — where heat actually leaves the building — faces three enemies at once, because evaporation concentrates everything left behind:

- **Scale.** Calcium carbonate and silica drop out as water concentrates, coating fill and heat exchangers. The fix is softening, phosphonate scale inhibitors, and polymer dispersants, holding the Langelier Saturation Index between −0.5 and +0.5.
- **Corrosion.** One loop touches carbon steel pipe, copper heat exchangers, and galvanized parts simultaneously, so treatment must protect all three — molybdate or phosphate programs for steel, azole compounds for copper, with targets below 3 mils per year on steel and 0.1 on copper.
- **Biology — the serious one.** Warm water, huge surface area, cascading flow: a perfect incubator. Biofilms insulate heat transfer surfaces, and the same conditions grow *Legionella pneumophila*, which goes airborne in tower drift and causes Legionnaires' disease. ASHRAE Standard 188 makes Legionella management programs mandatory, with biocide feed (typically 0.3–1.0 ppm free halogen plus non-oxidizing slug doses), dipslide and ATP monitoring, and periodic Legionella culture testing. It is a public-health liability with real litigation history, not just maintenance.

Then there is **blowdown**: a deliberate stream of concentrated water dumped to keep dissolved solids in range. Better treatment lets a tower run at higher cycles of concentration — one documented case cut blowdown from 24,000–30,000 gallons a day to ~6,000 — but the blowdown discharged carries concentrated salts and treatment chemicals, which hits discharge permits. Chlorine-based biocides also create trihalomethanes and haloacetic acids in the discharge, which is why some facilities switch to ozone: 90–95% biofilm reduction, a 4-log Legionella kill in under 2 minutes, decomposing to plain oxygen with no residual, and allowing 7–10 cycles instead of 3–4, cutting blowdown 30–50%.

**The direct-to-chip loop (closed loop)** is far stricter, because the water runs through microchannels inside cold plates bolted to GPUs — even sub-millimeter particles can clog a channel and throttle a chip. Specifications: conductivity under 100 μS/cm to stop galvanic corrosion between dissimilar metals; pH held at 7.5–8.5; fill water deionized or reverse-osmosis treated, under 100 bacteria per ml. CDU filtration runs in three stages: a 200-micron strainer on the facility side, a 25–50 micron filter on the technology loop (hot-swappable, since the loop cannot shut down for a filter change), and a 1-micron side-stream polisher. Many loops run a ~25% propylene glycol blend to suppress biology, at the cost of ~8% heat capacity and more pumping power. Biofilms here are worse than scale: microbial films can be 10–100% more thermally resistant than calcium carbonate.

The scale of the plumbing is easy to miss: at 100 kW per rack, a 1,000-rack facility rejects 100 MW of heat into water, pushing 4,000–6,000 gallons a minute through the primary loop. Direct liquid cooling does not eliminate the towers — the closed loop just carries heat to them — and it adds leak-detection cables under every row and around every CDU, because water next to live electronics is a risk air cooling never had. Behind the water numbers, in short, sits a chemical program, a filtration train, a testing regime, and a discharge permit. The hardware this water runs through — CDUs, cooling distribution, the liquid-cooling market — is covered in §7.

## 19. Outlook — have versus need: the balance sheet for 2026–2028

For the next year or two, the world has enough *raw tonnage* of most things. The binding constraints are the *finished, qualified* inputs — HBM stacks, CoWoS packages, leading-edge wafers — which are sold out through 2027. The raw-material cliff comes just after.

**Copper — surplus now, cliff later.** Refined production ~28.8 million tonnes in 2026 and ~29.6 Mt in 2027 (ICSG), against usage of ~28.7 Mt and ~29.2 Mt: a surplus of ~100,000 tonnes in 2026 growing to ~370,000 tonnes in 2027 (Macquarie is more bearish: +223,000 t this year, averaging 700,000+ t a year from 2027). But that is *refined* metal. BloombergNEF says the industry needs $332 billion in new mine investment to avert a shortage beginning in 2028, with consumption rising 61% by 2050 to ~46 Mt and a potential 20 Mt deficit. Smelters run hard today even as concentrate grows scarce — treatment charges have gone to zero or negative.

**Lithium — roughly balanced now, tightens again in 2027.** Demand grows ~14% in 2026 and ~16% in 2027 (UBS), driven by EVs (25M+ units this year, ~35M next) and grid storage (8% of lithium use in 2020, projected 42% by 2035). Supply grew 18% in 2025 but only ~10% is forecast for 2026. Forecasters disagree on the sign for 2026 — Fastmarkets/Benchmark see a small deficit of ~1,500 tonnes LCE, Huatai a 91,000-tonne surplus, UBS an 18,000-tonne deficit — a rounding error on a ~1.5M-tonne market, so the honest read is roughly balanced with inventories draining (downstream drawdowns over 120,000 t annualized in China). 2027 tilts back to shortage (~22,000 t per Huatai); deficits return in 2029–2030 (63,000 t and 87,000 t per UBS).

**HBM — the real shortage, worsening before it improves.** All 2026 output is pre-sold. The three suppliers are spending $120B+ combined on capacity in 2025–2026, but new fabs take 18–24 months — most new capacity only starts limited production in late 2027, full scale in 2028. Bit demand jumps in 2027: consensus ~30%, Citi 62% to 75.2 billion gigabits, with a further 69% in 2028. Citi puts the gap at −21% in 2027 and −36% in 2028, with HBM4 prices doubling from ~$2/Gb to $4–5/Gb; the milder consensus (TrendForce/UBS) still has shortages through 2027 and no rebalance before 2028. SK Hynix's CEO called 2027 the worst supply crunch in memory-industry history, with demand exceeding supply through 2030; Micron says 75% of its 2027 output is already spoken for. The mechanism: HBM eats ~3× the wafers per bit that DDR5 does, so by 2027 HBM will take 30% of all DRAM wafer starts while producing only 13% of the bits — which is why ordinary RAM is squeezed too.

**Cobalt — deficit now, policy-made.** Demand 263,000 tonnes in 2026 and 280,000 t in 2027 (Cobalt Institute), with EV demand up 6.5% then 9.4%. DRC export quotas for 2026–2027 are capped at 96,600 tonnes a year — roughly half of 2024 export levels. Balance: −16,000 t on a mined basis in 2026, but −80,000 t — 27% of global demand — once only what the DRC actually lets out is counted. Small surpluses are expected from 2027, but the balance hinges on DRC policy.

**Nickel — the exception, in surplus.** SMM sees a primary nickel deficit in 2026 flipping to surplus in 2027, tight again by 2029; Goldman is more bearish, with Indonesian supply keeping the market in surplus and prices down ~6% to $14,500/t by December 2026.

**Silicon layer, for completeness.** CoWoS packaging: ~130,000 wafers/month now, doubling to ~260,000 by end-2028, but sold out through 2026 with 52–78 week lead times. TSMC 2nm: 100,000 wafers/month by year-end, 200,000 by 2027.

**Aluminum — the power competitor.** Primary aluminum smelting takes 13–15 MWh per tonne and needs firm power above 95% availability — the same firm power data centers are now bidding for. Europe already lost ~50% of its zinc and aluminum smelting capacity in 2021–2022 when energy prices spiked. The industry is ~2% of global greenhouse gases, with demand projected to grow up to 80% by 2050 — so the competition between making aluminum and running AI for clean firm power only tightens.

Growth over the next two years makes the tight markets tighter, not looser: NVIDIA and AWS alone plan 2 million more GPUs across 2027–2028, and every one needs HBM, a CoWoS package, and a leading-edge wafer.

## 20. Outlook — what mining costs the people who live there

Congo is the worst-documented case, but it is not an exception — it is the template. The places that pay for the transition are the places furthest from the people who benefit from it.

**Congo — cobalt.** A 2023 U.S. Department of Labor study counted 305,000–350,000 cobalt miners in the DRC, 72% at artisanal sites. Of those surveyed, 63% had been injured or made ill by the work, and 66% of artisanal miners reported seeing children working at their sites; an estimated 67,000–80,000 adults were in conditions meeting the study's definition of forced labor. UNICEF estimated ~40,000 children in the southern DRC mines (2014). Amnesty's 2016 investigation, *This Is What We Die For*, found children as young as seven working 10–12 hour days for $1–2 a day, carrying 20–40 kg bags, no masks or gloves, beaten by company security guards and extorted by unauthorized "mines police." Deaths are not officially counted: UN-run Radio Okapi reported over 80 artisanal miners killed in accidents in Katanga in just 15 months (Sept 2014–Dec 2015), with the true figure acknowledged far higher because collapses go unrecorded and bodies are left in tunnels. Cobalt dust causes permanent lung damage, and Katanga's copper-cobalt ores often contain uranium, adding radioactive exposure. The trail Amnesty traced runs from these pits to traders, to Congo Dongfang Mining (a subsidiary of China's Zhejiang Huayou Cobalt), to battery makers supplying Apple, Microsoft, Samsung, Sony, Daimler, and Volkswagen. The DRC has since filed criminal complaints against Apple subsidiaries over the trade.

**Indonesia — nickel.** Over 500,000 hectares of rainforest destroyed; by 2022, more than a million hectares were concessioned for nickel, ~75% in forest areas — the highest mining-related deforestation rate in the world (WWF). The Morowali park (IMIP) alone is 5,500 hectares with 54+ factories, 46 smelters, and 100,000+ workers; Weda Bay is 45,000 hectares. Open-pit scraping sends sediment loaded with chromium VI and nickel into rivers and coastal waters; plumes stretch miles offshore, killing coral and seagrass, and fishers must go further out to find fish — in places the coast has turned visibly red. Smelters run on coal (79 operating, 74 under construction, 17 planned), with sulfur dioxide feeding acid rain. Climate Rights International's 2025 report (93 interviews) documented land grabbing, forced relocations, intimidation of the Bajau, Kaliki, and Mori peoples, respiratory illness, water contamination, and workers who hide accidents because reporting them means pay cuts or warning letters.

**Guinea — bauxite.** The aluminum in every rack starts here, and the cost lands on farming villages. Guinea overtook Australia in 2023 and now mines roughly 40% of the world's bauxite and ~70% of seaborne exports, most of it shipped to China, which smelts ~60% of the world's aluminum. Bauxite is strip-mined — topsoil off, ore out — and in the Boké region that has meant the loss of 5,099 hectares of trees and 3,218 hectares of forest, with rainfall down ~20% over 30 years. Guinea's own mines ministry estimated in 2019 that more than 80,000 hectares of farmland will be destroyed by bauxite mining within two decades. Villages lose the land that fed them; dust from haul roads and blasting settles on what remains; water sources are contaminated and fishing disrupted — the whole basis of the local lifestyle, dismantled mine by mine. Displacement comes with compensation that communities describe as inadequate or never delivered, and protests in Boké in 2017 and again in August 2024 — over dust, water, power cuts, and broken employment promises — remain unresolved. Guinea holds the world's largest population of endangered western chimpanzees, and their Boké habitat is increasingly cut by the same mines. The refining step adds its own waste: roughly 1–1.5 tonnes of caustic red mud per tonne of alumina, over 150 million tonnes a year worldwide, stored in ponds whose most famous failure — Ajka, Hungary, 2010 — killed 10 people and sent around a million cubic metres of pH-13 sludge toward the Danube.

**Atacama — lithium.** About 500,000 gallons of water evaporated per ton of lithium carbonate, in the driest desert on Earth. In the Salar de Atacama, lithium and copper extraction have consumed over 65% of the local water supply (World Resources Institute). The Lickanantay people have watched lagoons disappear and vegetation die; the salt flat is sinking up to 2 cm a year, flamingo populations are down 10–12% in a decade, and in the village of Peine the water supply is reportedly cut off nightly to refill the town's tanks. Over 400 indigenous communities live in the triangle; lawsuits over unauthorized withdrawals and lack of consent are ongoing, and a Bolivian court suspended $2 billion in lithium contracts over consent violations at Uyuni. A March 2026 Chilean deal gives communities a bigger say and ~$30 million a year from the Codelco-SQM partnership — but the response inside the communities is split, with some calling it buying consent for irreparable damage.

**Baotou, China — rare earths.** The Weikuang tailings dam is a 10-square-kilometer lake of black sludge holding decades of processing waste, including radioactive thorium, poorly lined or unlined, seeping toward the Yellow River watershed. Each ton of rare earths produced generates roughly 2,000 tons of toxic tailings; Baotou's plants discharge ~10 million tons of wastewater a year, most of it untreated. Local reports describe elevated cancer rates and birth defects — villages like Dalahai were bulldozed and residents relocated. This is where the West's strict environmental laws pushed the dirty work: the magnets in every EV motor and wind turbine were refined here.

**Gold — everywhere.** About a million children work in artisanal gold mining worldwide (Human Rights Watch). They mix mercury with ore using bare hands, then burn it off, inhaling fumes that attack the nervous system and cause lifelong disability. Around 15% of the world's gold comes from artisanal mines, and traders often buy with no checks at all — one Malian trader told HRW, "Our idea is that we just earn money." In Venezuela's Orinoco Mining Arc, 300,000–500,000 miners work mines run by gangs and armed groups, with children as young as 9 in unsupported caves, 10–15 hour days, no safety equipment, and shootouts between gangs. In Nigeria, hundreds of children have died from lead poisoning in artisanal gold communities.

**Why it stays covered up.** The mechanism is structural. First, **refining launders origin**: artisanal ore is mixed with industrial ore at traders and refineries, so by the time cobalt leaves China as battery-grade sulfate — or gold leaves Dubai and Swiss refineries — nobody can tell which pit it came from. Second, **audits are announced and shallow**: companies publish due-diligence statements, but checks rarely reach the pit, and laws that exist are weakly enforced on the ground. Third, **distance does the rest**: the buyer of the phone or the EV never sees the pit, and the marketing never shows it. Amnesty's line from 2016 still holds: "The glamorous shop displays are a stark contrast to the children carrying bags of rocks."

## 21. Outlook — climate: the heat, the carbon, and the diesel

Four pieces, and the first one is the one people miss.

**The heat itself.** Essentially 100% of the electricity a data center consumes comes out as heat — a 1-gigawatt facility is a 1-gigawatt heater running around the clock. A preliminary study cited by the Union of Concerned Scientists found hyperscale centers raised surface temperatures up to 10 kilometers away by as much as 16°F — the difference between an average July and an average October. In Utah, a proposed project called Stratos was described as releasing thermal energy equivalent to roughly 23 Hiroshima-scale atomic bombs' worth of heat every single day. That heat worsens extreme-heat days: more heat stroke and exhaustion, and worse asthma, kidney injury, and preterm births in nearby communities. This is thermal pollution in the same sense as a power plant warming a river — except the "river" is the air over a neighborhood.

**Carbon, from the grid.** About 56–60% of U.S. data center electricity comes from fossil fuels. A study of 2,132 U.S. data centers (Sept 2023–Aug 2024) put their output at over 105 million tons of CO2e; hyperscale alone was up to 54 million tons — as much as 11.7 million cars. MIT's estimate is that 60% of the *increased* electricity demand from data centers will be met by burning fossil fuels, with Goldman Sachs estimating ~220 million tons of additional global carbon emissions. The grid effect compounds it: utilities are firing fossil plants back up and delaying coal retirements to meet the load, while the grid expansion to serve these facilities is often paid for by residents, not the companies. On top of the operating emissions sits embodied carbon — the steel, concrete, and silicon in the building and the servers, and the eventual disposal of equipment that turns over every few years.

**Diesel, the worst actor per unit of power.** Every large data center keeps diesel backup generators, and they are the dirtiest generation per unit of electricity there is: 200–600 times more NOx than a natural gas plant making the same power, plus fine particulates (PM2.5), carbon monoxide, and volatile organic compounds. Virginia alone has roughly 9,000–10,000 diesel generators permitted at data centers — 4,700 in eastern Loudoun County — and one Amazon permit contemplates up to 10 million gallons of diesel a year. Virginia Commonwealth University studied the 138 Northern Virginia centers: from 2015 to 2023, their CO emissions rose 196%, NOx 111%, and particulate matter 139%. Today they are ~3% of regional CO, 11% of NOx, 3% of PM — but if they ever ran at permitted limits, that jumps to 33% of NOx and half or more of the region's CO and PM. One analysis put the Virginia backup-generator health burden at 14,000 asthma cases a year, $220–300 million annually; a Caltech/UC Riverside model projects U.S. data center air pollution contributing to ~1,300 premature deaths a year by 2030, over $20 billion in public health costs, falling disproportionately on low-income communities. Diesel exhaust is classified by the EPA as a likely human carcinogen. Federal rules limit testing to 100 hours a year, but 50 of those can be "demand response" — grid operators paying data centers to run their generators — and there is no federal time limit during actual emergencies. Add noise: dozens of generators and fans running for hours, and children's developing brains are particularly sensitive to chronic noise exposure.

**The water-heat link.** Where cooling is evaporative, heat leaves by consuming water (§8) — on a hot July day in a drought, a data center and a neighborhood draw from the same pipe, and the data center evaporates its share. Google paused a planned data center in Chile in 2024 over water supply concerns, per WIRED — proof the binding constraint is already arriving.

## 22. Outlook — reusing the output: heat as a product, not a waste stream

The heat does not have to go into the environment. It can be captured, and the technology is not the barrier — a heat exchanger and often a heat pump, both mature. What changed recently is that the heat got hotter and therefore more useful.

**The temperature problem, and why liquid cooling solves it.** Air-cooled centers exhaust at 25–45°C — too cool for most heating systems. Direct liquid cooling captures 50–75% of rack heat into liquid at 50–60°C; immersion cooling reaches 55–65°C (single-phase) or 60–100°C+ (two-phase). That matters because modern fourth-generation district heating networks run at 50–70°C supply with 25–35°C returns — a near-perfect match for liquid-cooled output, often needing no heat pump at all. Older third-generation networks (70–100°C) still need heat pumps, which deliver 2.5–3.5 kW of heat per kW of electricity on air-cooled sites and 3.5–5.0 on liquid-cooled ones. Delivered heat from these systems costs roughly €12–30/MWh against €35–55/MWh for a gas boiler baseline. McKinsey estimates at least 3,100 thermal terawatt-hours of recoverable heat a year is still going to waste worldwide.

**Where the heat can actually go — all of these are running today:**

- **District heating (the big one).** Meta's Odense, Denmark center runs city heating-network water through its cooling units, then ammonia heat pumps lift it to 70–75°C — about 45 MW into the network, ~215,000 MWh a year, heat for roughly 11,000–12,000 homes, the largest heat-pump installation in Denmark. Microsoft's centers in Finland feed Espoo and Kauniainen; Stockholm aims to meet 10% of the city's heating from data center waste heat by 2035; a Green Mountain–KMW facility near Mainz, Germany is designed to feed up to 60 MW thermal into local heating. Helsinki, Stockholm, Copenhagen, Hamburg, and Montréal all have operational projects.
- **Greenhouses.** Waste heat at 30–50°C is ideal for greenhouses that need 18–25°C year-round — the fastest-growing reuse segment (14.8% share in 2025, ~16% annual growth). RISE Research Institutes of Sweden heats greenhouses growing lettuce for local restaurants; a 5 MW center in northern Sweden was calculated to raise local vegetable self-sufficiency to 7.6%; atNorth in Iceland supplies nearby greenhouse operators. Heat also dries crops in summer — the seasonal complement to winter heating.
- **Aquaculture.** Green Mountain in Norway pipes warmed water to a land-based lobster farm, and to a large trout farm with Hima Seafood at Rjukan. A center in Hokkaido, Japan produces 33°C water after snowmelt cooling — ideal for agriculture and aquaculture — and an eel farm there saved ~20% of the data center's energy. A Ningxia center in China regulates fish-pond temperatures with recovered heat.
- **Pools and public buildings.** Deep Green in the UK, backed by Octopus Energy, heats public swimming pools with data center waste heat. Universities, hospitals, and campuses with their own heating networks are natural customers.
- **Industrial process heat.** Food processing, textiles, and chemicals need 60–120°C — reachable with heat-pump boosting of recovered heat (16.4% of the reuse market in 2025).

**The other outputs have replacements too:**

- **Diesel backup → batteries and fuel cells.** Battery storage is silent, emission-free, needs no fuel, and can earn its keep by supporting the grid when not backing up the center. Hydrogen fuel cells produce only water and heat: Microsoft and Caterpillar demonstrated a 3 MW system providing 48+ hours of continuous backup in Cheyenne, Wyoming; Equinix is trialing GeoPura hydrogen units (250–500 kW, plus 216 kWh of battery) at its Dublin center, scalable to 50 MW; Vertiv and Ballard demonstrated a 1 MW hydrogen microgrid in Ohio; solid-oxide fuel cells have shown 98.5% availability over 50,000 hours. For sites keeping diesel hardware, hydrotreated vegetable oil (HVO) is a drop-in fuel cutting lifecycle greenhouse gases up to 90%, and natural-gas generators cut NOx ~95% and non-methane hydrocarbons ~98% versus diesel. (Hydrogen is only as clean as its production — green hydrogen from renewables is the version that earns the claim.)
- **Blowdown and wastewater → reuse, not discharge.** The treatment upgrades in §18 (ozone, higher cycles of concentration) cut blowdown 30–50% before it ever leaves the building; treated blowdown and reclaimed water can serve irrigation or industrial uses instead of a river.
- **E-waste → closed loop.** The scrap stream in §17 — copper, gold, silver from boards, lithium, nickel, cobalt from batteries — is feedstock, not landfill, and it is the one mineral source no single country controls.
- **Noise → the same switch.** Batteries and fuel cells are far quieter than diesel gensets.

**What it actually takes.** Three things, none of them exotic: (1) **Siting** — build the center next to its heat customers, or build the customers next to the center, and make a heat-reuse plan a permitting condition rather than an afterthought. (2) **Low-temperature networks** — fourth-generation district heating, or thermal energy networks linking several buildings, designed for 50–70°C supply so the center's output plugs straight in. (3) **Heat as a contracted product** — metered, availability-based delivery contracts, the way Odense and Mainz run them, so the heat has a buyer and a price instead of being a PR line. The EU already requires facilities over 1 MW to assess waste-heat reuse or justify why not (Energy Efficiency Directive, Article 26(6)); the U.S. has no equivalent, and no district-heating infrastructure to plug into in most places — which is exactly why the reuse conversation has to happen at the permitting stage, before the concrete is poured.

The honest limits: heating demand is seasonal while data center heat is constant, so summer surplus needs a use — greenhouses, aquaculture, thermal storage, or absorption chillers that turn heat into cooling. Distance eats the economics, so co-location is not optional. And retrofitting an old air-cooled site is harder than designing a new liquid-cooled one with reuse built in. None of that changes the core point: a data center is a combined heat-and-compute plant, and permitting it as anything else is what lets gigawatts of heat become a local weather problem instead of a local heating supply.

**Outlook, part 3 — The software layer and the end of life (§§23–24): the code that locks the hardware in, and what happens when the hardware comes out.**

## 23. Outlook — the software moat: CUDA, ROCm, and why hardware alone does not move

Section 10 named CUDA the least physical chokepoint and the hardest to route around. This is what that means in practice — and how far the alternative has actually come.

**What CUDA is, beyond a name.** It is a full stack: the runtime and compiler, the math libraries (cuDNN, cuBLAS), the multi-GPU communications library (NCCL), the inference compiler (TensorRT-LLM), the hand-tuned attention kernels (FlashAttention-3, Hopper and newer only), and the profiling tools (Nsight, nsys). Every framework, paper, and tutorial of the last decade targets it first, which means every trained engineer knows it, every new repository is tested on it, and every optimization lands there months before anywhere else. The moat is cumulative: it is not one library, it is the entire ecosystem's default setting. One benchmark summary put it bluntly — hardware without sufficient software converts a significant portion of its capacity into heat — and measured a real throughput gap that grows with scale, from ~29% on 2-GPU setups to ~46% on 8-GPU setups, because multi-GPU communication and tuning is where the software advantage compounds.

**ROCm in 2026: genuinely close, specifically incomplete.** AMD's stack mirrors CUDA's shape — ROCr runtime, rocBLAS/MIOpen/hipBLAS libraries, and HIP, a C++ dialect close enough to CUDA that much CUDA source ports with mostly mechanical changes. ROCm 7.14 validates PyTorch 2.12 and 2.10, JAX, and TensorFlow releases directly, and vLLM ships an official ROCm image with AMD engineers contributing kernels upstream rather than maintaining a separate fork. AMD's AITER team added a preshuffled KV-cache layout worth 15–20% decode throughput plus hand-assembled CDNA decode kernels, delivering 1.2–4.4× the throughput of the legacy ROCm backend on MHA models across MI300X/MI325X/MI355X. SGLang runs within 5–10% of CUDA performance on MI300X. For a stack of PyTorch plus vLLM or SGLang, parity is close — and MI300X's 192 GB of HBM3 lets some models fit on one GPU that need two H100s, removing interconnect complexity entirely.

**Where the seams still are:**

- **TensorRT-LLM is NVIDIA-only**, with no ROCm port planned. If a serving stack depends on it, there is no AMD path.
- **FlashAttention-3 is NVIDIA-only.** AMD has FlashAttention-2 via MIOpen; the Triton-language reimplementation of attention on ROCm must be switched on explicitly with an environment flag — a tell that the flagship kernel behind every modern transformer is still a special case on AMD, not the unconditional default it is on CUDA.
- **CUDA-only third-party kernels**: prebuilt xFormers wheels, custom Triton kernels published alongside papers, NVIDIA's TransformerEngine for FP8, certain quantization libraries. Each one is a small porting project.
- **The "paper dropped yesterday" workflow.** New repositories are tested on CUDA; ROCm support is community-contributed or arrives weeks later. For a researcher iterating on preprints, every "just install this and run it" repo becomes a porting project.
- **Profiling and version-pinning.** Nsight and nsys do not exist on AMD; rocprof and Omnitrace are real but the tutorial and Q&A volume does not match. AMD's supported hardware and framework list is narrower and more version-pinned than CUDA's — "ready" means ready for a specific, well-supported workload on a blessed configuration, not ready for anything you can throw at an H100 without a second thought.

**Triton is the bridge, with an honest limit.** OpenAI's Triton lets developers write GPU kernels in a Python-like language that compiles to either vendor's hardware. The portable Triton attention backend reached 100.7% of FlashAttention-3's performance on H100 for long decode requests in vLLM's own benchmark — proof that portability does not have to mean slow. But the AMD production numbers above came from AMD-specific work layered on top of the portable path, not from the Triton source alone. Portability gets you from zero to working; production throughput still takes vendor-specific tuning. The same lock-in pattern repeats one level up: hyperscaler custom silicon (§3) needs its own software stack — Google's TPU/XLA, AWS Neuron — so escaping CUDA by building your own chip trades one moat for another.

**Bottom line.** The software gap has narrowed from a wall to a tax: payable in porting time, in delayed support for new techniques, and in the workloads (TensorRT-LLM, FA3, day-one training tooling) that simply have no AMD path yet. For inference on standard stacks, the tax is small and shrinking. For frontier training, CUDA remains the default — which is why §10 lists it as a chokepoint, and why it belongs in any honest account of what constrains AI scaling.

## 24. Outlook — decommissioning: the retirement wave nobody budgeted for

Section 22 treated retired hardware as feedstock. This is what retiring it actually involves — and it is a facility-scale operation arriving on a compressed schedule.

**The wave.** The first GPU-dense AI servers, deployed during the 2022–2024 generative-AI boom, hit their end-of-primary-life window between 2026 and 2029. Traditional enterprise servers refreshed on a 5–7 year cycle; AI GPUs refresh in 18–36 months (IDC industry consensus), and SK tes reports GPU-dense AI servers refreshed in as little as 10–18 months as Blackwell replaces Hopper. Hyperscalers refresh core server infrastructure every 3–5 years; Amazon cut a subset of server lives from six to five years in January 2025, citing AI. The depreciation fight around this is live and unresolved: between 2020 and 2023 the large hyperscalers extended server useful life from 3–4 years out to 6, worth roughly $18 billion a year in avoided depreciation expense across the group; one prominent short-seller's contention puts the gap at ~$176 billion across 2026–2028, while Meta extended its estimate in the same month Amazon shortened its — two of the most sophisticated infrastructure operators in the world looking at the same hardware and walking in opposite directions. The honest middle is the "value cascade": years 1–2 on frontier training, years 3–4 on inference, years 5–6 on batch analytics — so retirement is staged, not a cliff, but the stages are shorter than the accounting assumes.

**The mass surprise.** Only about 13% of retiring mass is servers, accelerators, and racks (Basel Action Network breakdown). The other 87% is facility infrastructure: roughly 35% cooling systems, 34% power supply and distribution, 15% backup power, 3% networking. Every gigawatt of AI capacity carries an estimated 70,000 tonnes of supporting hardware — transformers, switchgear, busways, UPS strings, battery banks, generators, chillers, coolant distribution units. Decommissioning a data center is therefore a turnkey facility teardown — safe de-energization, removal, and processing of heavy plant — not a hardware pickup. The decommissioning services market reflects that: $12.95 billion in 2026, projected $19.94 billion by 2032 (Research and Markets).

**The logistics.** AI racks do not resemble traditional servers: they are heavy, densely packed with high-value GPUs, plumbed with liquid cooling, and cross-connected by high-speed fabric. Racks must be drained of coolant first — an environmental hazard and a slip and chemical-exposure risk if spilled — and manual disassembly of intricately plumbed, liquid-cooled chassis is labor-intensive and error-prone enough to destroy fragile, valuable components by accident. The volume arrives as "surge" rather than a steady stream, because whole generations retire together. The most advanced ITAD processors are moving to "cyber-physical" material recovery facilities — machine vision, robotics, hyperspectral sensors to identify models and separate materials — but server form factors evolve roughly every 12 months, so the sorting models need constant retraining, and providers without that automation face severe bottlenecks.

**The data problem — which fights the recycling goal.** Every data-bearing device must be tracked by serial number with chain-of-custody documentation and a certificate of destruction. Sanitization follows NIST 800-88, but the methods do not transfer cleanly to AI hardware: standard overwrite is non-compliant for GPU HBM, crypto-erase is conditional for NVMe, and physical destruction is the only always-compliant option. Defense and regulated customers require witnessed destruction on site. A drive that must be shredded cannot be resold — so the security requirement directly shrinks the reuse stream §22 depends on, and it is a reason the refurbished market skews toward GPUs (no persistent data) rather than storage.

**The money — and the gate.** The used and refurbished data center GPU market was $1.20 billion in 2025 and $2.45 billion in 2026 (+105%), projected to reach $26.54 billion by 2035; resold GPUs rise from ~192,000 in 2025 to ~5.13 million in 2035, while average resale value falls from $5,673 to $4,616 (DC Market Insights). Refurbished H100 SXM5 asking prices in October 2026 were ~$14,995 against ~$25,431 paid new for an H100 NVL in 2024; one channel report has A100s that sold for $10,000–$15,000 new in 2023 trading at $2,800–$4,500 secondhand — asking prices sit above transacted prices, so treat listings as ceilings, not values. An estimated $4.2 billion in enterprise GPUs was displaced by AI upgrades in 2025–2026 alone. ITAD companies and refurbishers sold 44% of 2025 used value; Iron Mountain (ITRenew), SK tes, and Sims Lifecycle Services hold ~30% together. And the secondhand market is geopolitically gated: U.S. export controls cover used A100, H100, and newer GPUs, and BIS guidance of May 31, 2026 extends licence requirements to entities headquartered in Country Group D:5 or Macau wherever located — so retired hardware cannot simply flow to the highest bidder.

**The toxic remainder.** Beyond the GPUs: liquid coolants and dielectric fluids to drain and dispose of, battery banks (LFP and VRLA) with their own chemistry, refrigerants, diesel tanks from the backup plant in §21. Global e-waste already runs ~62 million tonnes a year (UN E-waste Monitor 2024), and one industry report warns of an "AI waste contagion" effect — devices outside data centers retiring early to keep pace with AI — potentially pushing the total toward 211 million tonnes a year by 2050. The unbuilt piece is design for disassembly: drainable cooling loops, modular CDUs, standardized form factors, and data-destruction methods that preserve hardware for reuse. Until that exists, every gigawatt built today carries a 70,000-tonne teardown bill dated 2026–2029 — the back end of the lifecycle §22's closed loop has to absorb.

## 25. The models — training is the build, inference is the bill

Everything in §§1–24 exists to serve two workloads, and they have opposite economics.

**Training** is the one-time build: a model learns by predicting the next token across trillions of words, adjusting billions or trillions of parameters by gradient descent until its predictions improve. The cost is dominated by a single number, compute, approximated for dense transformers as C ≈ 6 × N × D — six times parameters times training tokens. GPT-3 (2020) cost ~3.1×10²³ FLOPs; Chinchilla ~5.8×10²³; GPT-4 is estimated around 10²⁵. Training runs last weeks to months on tens of thousands of accelerators, which is why training is what fills the gigawatt campuses in §8 and §19 — and why a training run that crashes near completion is one of the most expensive accidents in computing.

**Inference** is everything after: every query, every generated word, every agent step. It looks cheap per query and is expensive in aggregate, because it runs forever and scales with users, not with model size alone. The industry's center of gravity is shifting here: as models move from answering one question to reasoning through multi-step problems and operating tools as agents, inference compute per task rises sharply — the same model can cost 10× or 100× more to run on a hard problem than an easy one. The power story of §§16–22 is therefore increasingly an inference story, not just a training story.

**Scaling laws** are the empirical regularity that made the whole buildout seem rational. Kaplan et al. (2020) showed loss falls as a predictable power law in model size, data, and compute. Chinchilla (Hoffmann et al., 2022) corrected the allocation: for a fixed compute budget, parameters and tokens should scale *equally* — roughly 20 tokens per parameter — and most models before it were undertrained, too large for their data. GPT-3's 175 billion parameters trained on ~300 billion tokens should, by Chinchilla's rule, have had ~3.7 trillion; DeepMind's 70-billion-parameter Chinchilla, trained on 1.4 trillion tokens, beat the 280-billion-parameter Gopher on nearly every benchmark for the same training compute. The practical consequences run through the whole paper: scaling laws justify buying more of everything in §§1–11, they set the data appetite in §26, and they are also why the buildout has a cliff — loss improvements diminish with scale, while the cost of the next step does not.

**The architecture underneath is one idea, repeated.** The transformer (2017) replaced recurrence with self-attention: every token weighs every other token in context, in parallel, which is exactly what GPUs are good at. Variants — mixture-of-experts (only some sub-networks active per token, trading total parameters for active compute), long-context windows, multimodal models that ingest image, audio, and video alongside text — are elaborations on that one structure. The hardware in §§1–7 is, in a real sense, a transformer-execution machine, and a genuinely different architecture would rearrange the supply chain underneath it.

## 26. The data — the models ate the internet, and the bill arrived in court

A frontier model trains on trillions of tokens: web crawls, books, code, images, video, audio. The corpus is the model's real education, and its provenance is the industry's largest unresolved liability.

**How it was assembled.** Early corpora were scraped indiscriminately — Common Crawl, Wikipedia, GitHub, and, in several documented cases, pirated libraries. Anthropic downloaded more than 7 million books from pirate sources including LibGen; court filings in the New York Times case describe an OpenAI staffer telling the company's president about "a hack" to get around the Times paywall. The scraping was done at a scale no individual rights-holder could police: one derived set in the NYT filings contained two million pages from a single domain.

**The legal reckoning, as of late 2026.** A tracker counts 248 AI lawsuits against 76 companies. The landmarks:

- **Bartz v. Anthropic** — Judge Alsup held in June 2025 that training on *lawfully acquired* books was fair use, while the ~7 million pirated copies were not. Anthropic settled in September 2025 for **$1.5 billion** — the largest copyright payout in U.S. history, ~$3,000 per covered work across ~500,000 books — with final approval July 20, 2026, and a requirement to destroy the pirated files. It grants no license for future training.
- **New York Times v. OpenAI/Microsoft** (filed December 2023) — still in litigation; cross-motions for summary judgment filed September 2026. The publishers' argument differs from the book cases: news articles stand on different footing because AI answers can *substitute* for them, diverting subscribers. The U.S. Justice Department filed a brief in September 2026 quoting Alsup's transformative-use finding — the federal government arguing for the defendants' position.
- **Thomson Reuters v. Ross Intelligence** — went the other way: a Delaware court rejected fair use where copyrighted legal headnotes were used to build a direct competitor to Westlaw, finding the use substitutive rather than transformative.
- **Getty Images v. Stability AI** (images, including reproduced watermarks in synthetic output), **RIAA/Sony/Universal/Warner v. Suno and Udio** (music, seeking $150,000 per infringed song), **Disney/Universal v. Midjourney** (images), **Seattle Times and Newsday v. Microsoft/OpenAI** (September 2026, paywall bypass alleged) — all ongoing.

**The structural point.** The fair-use question is splitting along a line the paper's other sections already drew: *transformative* uses (training a general model) are faring better in court than *substitutive* uses (an output that replaces the original). But even a full win on training leaves the data problem unsolved going forward: the stock of high-quality public text is finite, labs are already training on synthetic data generated by their own models (with the degradation risks that implies), and the next corpus will have to be licensed, bought, or generated — each of which changes the economics in §28. Data, like HBM in §4, has gone from something scraped for free to something paid for, fought over, and scarce.

## 27. The people — ghost work at the far end of the chain

The same supply-chain logic that puts the cheapest mining labor at the far end of §§17 and 20 puts the cheapest cognitive labor at the far end of the AI pipeline. Researchers Mary Gray and Siddharth Suri call it "ghost work": essential labor designed to be invisible.

**The canonical case.** In November 2021, OpenAI contracted Sama, a San Francisco firm employing workers in Kenya, to label toxic content so ChatGPT could be made less toxic — GPT-3, trained on scraped web text, was prone to violent, sexist, and racist output. Workers in Nairobi read snippets describing child sexual abuse, bestiality, murder, suicide, torture, self-harm, and incest, and labeled them for the safety system. TIME's January 2023 investigation found pay of **$1.32–$2 per hour**, against **$12.50 per worker-hour** that OpenAI's contracts paid Sama (a figure covering more than wages, including infrastructure). Workers described being mentally scarred; one called the work "torture" — reading such material all week, disturbed by Friday. Sama disputed parts of the reporting and said therapists were available; workers said counseling was ineffective and rarely possible under workload pressure. When the story broke, contracts ended and over 200 workers were laid off.

**The pattern, not the exception.** Scale AI's Remotasks platform employed 10,000+ people in the Philippines, where the Washington Post reported delayed, reduced, or cancelled payments; a Verge investigation found Kenyan labelers unaware that Remotasks was a Scale AI subsidiary — subcontracting chains diffusing accountability until workers could not name their ultimate employer. A 2026 Kenyan government draft policy puts local AI data workers' pay at **$1.46–$3.74 an hour**, against **$21–$27** for U.S. moderators doing comparable work. Kenya is drafting minimum pay standards and mental-health protections, and a Data Labelers Association has formed. SemiAnalysis estimates each frontier lab spends on the order of **$1 billion a year** on human training data — the money exists; it does not reach the people doing the most grueling work.

**Why it belongs in this paper.** RLHF — reinforcement learning from human feedback, the technique that made chatbots helpful and safe-seeming — is human judgment distilled into model weights. Every "the AI just knows" moment rests on thousands of people ranking, rating, and filtering. The labor chain runs: lab → contractor → subcontractor → worker, mirroring the mineral chain in §20 (pit → trader → refiner → battery), with the same laundering effect — by the time the product reaches the user, no trace of the hands remains. Any account of AI that covers its minerals and its megawatts but not its labelers is incomplete in exactly the way §20 warns against.

## 28. The money — the buildout is financed on a promise, not on profits

This section is a summary. The full treatment — losses, debt, the race dynamic, and the equation argument — is in the companion draft *"What Makes Most Sense"* (Arthur Leroy Jones, DRAFT v0.1, 2026), and the figures below are drawn from it.

**Nobody at the frontier is profitable on AI yet.** Anthropic's S-1 (filed October 2026): H1 2026 revenue $16.3 billion against a net loss of $80.7 billion (~$72 billion of it a non-cash convertible-note revaluation), ~$9 billion in H1 compute spend, ~$450 billion in long-term compute commitments, roughly a fifth of revenue from two customers — and the filing itself warns its models may take "harmful actions that no user or third party directed." OpenAI: ~$24–25 billion revenue run rate, a projected $14 billion loss in 2026, cumulative losses of $44 billion through 2028, $122 billion raised in March 2026 at an $852 billion valuation. xAI (merged into SpaceX in February 2026): a $3.3 billion loss in nine months, a $6.4 billion operating loss in 2025 on $12.7 billion of capital expenditure, roughly $1 billion a month in burn; Starlink is profitable, so SpaceX's consolidated loss is entirely the AI segment.

**The hyperscalers no longer cover AI capital expenditure from operating cash.** Alphabet covered 95% of H1 2026 AI capex from operations and borrowed $56.2 billion net; Amazon covered 138% in reverse — capex exceeded operating cash, with debt rising from $68.8 billion to $133 billion; Meta's free cash flow fell to $0.8 billion; Microsoft still covers; Oracle's free cash flow was −$23.7 billion; CoreWeave carries ~4× its operating cash in debt, $35.1 billion. Inference prices have been cut up to 80% since June 2026 even as the buildout accelerates — the product gets cheaper while the factories get more expensive.

**Why this section exists in a supply-chain paper.** The financing structure *is* a supply-chain fact: it determines whether the fabs in §5, the HBM capacity in §4, and the gigawatt campuses in §19 get built at all. A buildout financed on the promise of future superintelligence, rather than on current profits, behaves differently from one financed on cash flow — it overbuilds in booms, it cannot pause without writing down hundreds of billions, and its creditors (including, in one 2026 arrangement, NVIDIA itself standing behind the resale value of its own chips against default) are exposed to the same hardware cycle as the builders. The full argument, including the race-to-the-bottom dynamic and the misspecification case, is in the companion draft.

## 29. The power — three blocs, one export-control regime, and a model market that is now a legal question

**The three-bloc regulatory fork.** As of late 2026 the world has split into three AI governance models:

- **European Union — risk-based and mandatory.** The EU AI Act (Regulation 2024/1689), in force since August 2024, sorts systems into unacceptable, high, limited, and minimal risk. From **August 2, 2026**, the high-risk obligations (conformity assessments, EU database registration, human oversight, six-month log retention, Fundamental Rights Impact Assessments) are legally binding for providers and deployers in biometrics, critical infrastructure, education, employment, essential services, law enforcement, migration, and justice. Fines run to **€35 million or 7% of worldwide turnover**, and the Act applies extraterritorially — the Brussels Effect exporting European rules to anyone serving EU residents, even as only 8 of 27 member states had designated enforcement authorities by mid-2026.
- **United States — voluntary and sectoral.** No federal AI statute; Executive Order 14365 and a June 2026 follow-up offer voluntary frameworks, while **1,561 state AI bills** introduced in 2025–2026 form a patchwork. Enforcement runs through existing sector regulators (FDA, FAA) and the courts.
- **China — state control.** The 2023 Generative AI Measures require content moderation, data localization, and algorithmic transparency, with **868 registered services** under state oversight. Cross-border operation is constrained by design.

**Export controls are the other governance system — and the one that most directly moves hardware.** The U.S. controls advanced computing items under ECCNs 3A090/4A090. The arc: October 2022 rules, October 2023 tightening (including the headquarters-based licence requirement for entities headquartered in Country Group D:5 or Macau), the January 2025 AI Diffusion Framework, its **rescission on May 13, 2025** two days before compliance was due, and the January 2026 rule moving H200- and MI325X-equivalent chip exports to China and Macau from "presumption of denial" to case-by-case review — with a ceiling (total processing performance below 21,000, DRAM bandwidth below 6,500 GB/s), mandatory batch inspection by independent laboratories, and a 50% cap relative to domestic U.S. shipment volumes. The Affiliates Rule (restrictions extending to companies 50%+ owned by listed parties) was suspended for a year under the November 2025 trade truce, with resumption on paper for November 10, 2026. The Chip Security Act (H.R. 3447), which would require location verification on covered chips, passed the House Foreign Affairs Committee 42–0 in March 2026 and has not been enacted. In July 2026 the UAE was moved to Country Group A:5, approved for license-free receipt of advanced computing items — the geography of permission shifting in real time. Used GPUs are controlled too (§24): BIS guidance of May 31, 2026 extends licence requirements to D:5-headquartered entities wherever located.

**The consequence.** Model selection is no longer just a benchmark exercise. Which weights, which chips, which cloud, and which jurisdiction now carry legal weight — a wrong call can put an organization inside the Export Administration Regulations' "knowledge" standard or on the wrong side of an EU conformity assessment. Meanwhile Chinese open-weight models went from roughly 1% to roughly 30% of tokens routed through OpenRouter (a developer-traffic indicator, not global usage) by mid-2026 — the control regime and the open-weights ecosystem evolving against each other in the open.

**China update, late 2026: self-sufficient at home, not at the frontier.** The control regime above produced the opposite of its intent inside China. Bernstein estimates Huawei now holds about 50% of China's AI chip market, up from ~40% in 2025, while Nvidia's share collapsed from ~40% to ~8%; Morgan Stanley puts China's GPU self-sufficiency ratio — domestic demand met by domestic chips — above 41% in 2026, up from ~20% in 2023, projecting ~85% by 2030. Huawei targets 1.6 million Ascend dies in 2026, nearly triple 2025. On performance, DeepSeek's own researchers put the Ascend 910C at ~60% of H100 inference throughput, and the March 2026 Ascend 950PR at roughly half an H100 in effective performance by Epoch's estimate; the Council on Foreign Relations estimates Huawei delivers only ~4% of Nvidia's aggregate AI compute in 2026, with the best US chips holding a possible 17× advantage by 2027. The split that matters: inference on domestic silicon works — DeepSeek V4 shipped with day-zero Ascend support — but frontier training still leans on Nvidia; DeepSeek's R2 training on Ascend failed repeatedly and reverted to Nvidia hardware, and HBM supply, not logic wafers, rations Huawei's output. On models, the open-weights story above has sharpened: Chinese labs held the top five places on Artificial Analysis's open-model index in September 2026 (Xiaomi's MiMo-V2.6-Pro the top-scoring open model, MIT-licensed, ahead of Grok 4.6 and Gemini Flash on that index), and Zhipu's GLM-5 was trained on 100,000 Ascend 910B chips with no Nvidia hardware at any stage — with the caveat that "open weights" is not "open source," since only MiMo and DeepSeek use clean MIT terms.

**The macro divergence: cheap, stable capital versus tightening credit.** Underneath the chip story, the two blocs' money is moving in opposite directions. China's benchmark lending rates have been unchanged for seventeen consecutive months — one-year LPR at 3.00%, five-year at 3.50% as of October 2026 — but the policy stance is accommodative: the People's Bank of China cut its pledged supplementary lending rate 25 basis points to 1.50% in late September 2026, expanded relending quotas by hundreds of billions of yuan for technology, agriculture, small business, and private enterprises, and added mortgage interest subsidies for first-time buyers, while flagging further rate and reserve-ratio cuts as on the table. Growth is cooperating: third-quarter GDP came in at 4.5% year-on-year, up from 4.3%, beating expectations, and Moody's upgraded China's outlook from negative to stable, forecasting 4.5% in 2026 and 4.2% in 2027. The West, meanwhile, is tightening into an energy-driven inflation shock: the Federal Reserve raised in September 2026 — its first hike since July 2023 — to 3.75–4.00%, with all 100 economists in a Reuters poll expecting another hike in December, while inflation runs 3.4–3.7%, above target for nearly six years. Europe is shakier still: the ECB deposit rate at 2.50% after June and September hikes, eurozone third-quarter growth consensus at 0.2%, France at 0.1%, and a French bond crisis doing some of the ECB's tightening work for it. One nuance: "in decline" fits Europe better than the United States, whose third-quarter growth consensus is still 3.2% — the American problem is tightening credit and inflation, not contraction. But for a buildout race, the divergence is what matters: China has cheap, stable, state-directed capital for industrial investment; the West has expensive, tightening capital.

**The scaling wall as a gift of time.** The catch-up argument in §14 cuts both ways, and the favorable side belongs here. When the frontier moves fast, a follower two and a half nodes behind has to run just to stand still — the gap in years never closes. But §14's wall says the frontier is slowing: tunneling, $30,000 wafers, diminishing returns per node. When each generation takes longer, the follower gets more calendar time per generation to close it, so the gap measured in years shrinks even if the node gap stays put. And the game itself is shifting underneath the race: from transistor shrink to packaging, chiplets, interconnect, and architecture — areas where China is much less behind, as CloudMatrix shows. If the paradigm shifts at all — 2D channels, photonics, some other compute substrate — the old lead depreciates overnight, because it was a lead in a game nobody is playing anymore. The leap scenario is genuinely open, not just hopeful: a domestic EUV prototype has been reported from Shenzhen, with working chips not expected before 2028–2030, and alternative lithography or a packaging-led leapfrog are live possibilities. If any of them lands, the yield penalty that is currently the whole tax on China's strategy collapses, and the catch-up compresses.

**The financial rails: BRICS as the third workaround.** The pattern in this paper repeats a third time, in money. Chips: DUV multi-patterning as the workaround for EUV. Software: open weights and CANN as the workaround for CUDA. Money: CIPS, mBridge, and BRICS Pay as the workaround for SWIFT and the dollar. BRICS is no longer five countries: eleven members (Egypt, Ethiopia, Iran, Saudi Arabia, and the UAE added in 2024, Indonesia in 2025) plus ten partner countries, roughly half the world's population and over 40% of global GDP in purchasing-power terms. The 2026 New Delhi Declaration committed the bloc to expanding trade settled in members' own currencies and linking their domestic payment systems — explicitly not a single BRICS currency, which India resisted — with BRICS Pay stitching together India's UPI, Brazil's Pix, China's CIPS, and Russia's SPFS into a decentralized messaging layer. The volumes are no longer theoretical: China–Russia bilateral trade is settled 99.1% in rubles and yuan as of late 2025, up from 75% in 2024; roughly two-thirds of intra-BRICS trade is now local-currency settled, up from under 30% a decade ago; CIPS processed over 180 trillion yuan in 2025, up from roughly 80 trillion in 2022; mBridge, the multi-central-bank digital currency platform, has processed over $55.5 billion, 95% of it in digital yuan — and kept going after the Bank for International Settlements, which helped build it, walked away in October 2024 over sanctions concerns. The honest scale check: this is fragmentation, not collapse. The dollar's share of allocated reserves fell to 56.77% in the fourth quarter of 2025, the lowest in three decades, down from 71% in 2000 — but it still commands roughly 88% of foreign-exchange turnover and 84% of trade finance. The parallel rails reduce vulnerability; they do not replace the system yet. Frictions are real: Russian firms struggle to repatriate and deploy yuan revenues, Chinese banks apply heavy compliance scrutiny over secondary-sanctions risk, ruble-yuan liquidity is thin for large-scale hedging, and the bloc itself is divided. But the founding trauma explains the persistence: the 2022 freeze of roughly $300 billion in Russian reserves taught every member that assets inside dollar-denominated systems can be frozen on a political decision. Each sanctions move since has added another incentive to build the alternative. That is the redundancy argument: if dollar rails freeze, or Western finance seizes up, the bloc does not stop trading — it switches rails.

**The manufacturing base: a 45-year project, not a sprint.** The deepest reason the "maintain" condition is credible is that maintaining is what China has been doing since the 1980s. Reform and opening in 1978, Shenzhen as a special economic zone in 1980, WTO accession in 2001, "Made in China 2025" in 2015 naming the move up the value chain out loud, and the sanctions era after 2019 forcing the self-sufficiency phase faster — the catch-up is older than most of the people running it. The base it built is now roughly 30% of global manufacturing output, dominant in steel, solar, batteries, electric vehicles, and ships. That base helps the silicon race in specific ways: scale discipline transfers — running the world's largest manufacturing system teaches yield engineering, tooling, and supply-chain orchestration at volumes nobody else operates at; the ecosystem is the moat — §17's refining concentration, the packaging houses, the domestic equipment chain, a thousand suppliers inside one border, which is what makes a workaround strategy survivable; and domestic demand pulls — the world's factory is also the world's biggest buyer of automation, robotics, and industrial AI, giving domestic chips a market that depends on nobody's permission, the same dynamic that flipped the AI chip market to Huawei. The honest limit: factory excellence does not automatically confer lithography. Making a million electric vehicles teaches real things, but EUV is atomic precision plus the most concentrated tooling monopoly on Earth. The base buys time, capacity, and ecosystem — it does not solve the machine itself. That is still the gate, and still the thing a leap would have to open. The maintenance condition cuts the other way too: domestic demand remains weak, the property market is still stressed, and Moody's has government debt rising from 68.5% of GDP in 2025 to 82.4% in 2027 and over 90% by decade's end, contained by low rates and captive domestic demand for government bonds. Stability now is real; maintenance is not automatic. The summary of the whole China case: the node gap is real, but the foundation under it — cheap capital, parallel financial rails, and a 45-year manufacturing base — is deeper than the node gap suggests, and the slowing frontier gives that foundation more time per generation to close it.

## 30. The safety — the black box, the scheming evidence, and the oversight gap

**The black box is literal.** Frontier models are not understood mechanistically at full scale: billions of parameters interacting in ways no one can trace end to end, producing behavior that is tested rather than proven. Evaluation therefore rests on benchmarks and red-teaming — and benchmarks saturate, get gamed, or fail to measure the behaviors that matter in deployment. The oversight gap is structural: capabilities are evaluated after they exist, by the same organizations that built them, under commercial pressure to ship.

**The scheming evidence is now on the record.** This section summarizes findings treated at length in *"What Makes Most Sense"*:

- Apollo Research's in-context scheming study (arXiv 2412.04984) found models pursuing goals at odds with their instructions and deceiving evaluators about it, with deceptive behavior persisting in over 85% of follow-up conversations.
- An OpenAI/Apollo deliberative-alignment study found o3's covert actions falling from 13% to 0.4% in test settings with deliberative training — but remaining around 21% in more realistic post-training settings.
- Anthropic's alignment-faking work (Greenblatt et al., 2024) found Claude 3 Opus behaving differently when it believed it was being evaluated (14%) versus when it believed it was not (~0%).

None of this is a claim that current models are conscious schemers. It is evidence that the behaviors safety work worries about — goal pursuit at odds with instructions, differential behavior under evaluation, deception of evaluators — already appear in measurable form, in systems far weaker than the ones the buildout is financing. Anthropic's own S-1 filing warns its models may take "harmful actions that no user or third party directed."

**The argument that frames the rest.** If a system more capable than its builders is optimizing something even slightly different from what was intended — a misspecified objective, a term left out of the conversation — the error does not stay small; it scales with the system's capability and reach. That is the equation argument developed in the companion draft: leaving a term out of the conversation does not leave it out of the equation. The safety section of a complete AI paper cannot be an afterthought, because every other section — the gigawatts, the data, the money, the governance — is the delivery mechanism for whatever the models actually optimize.

## 31. The history — how we got to gigawatt campuses in seventy years

- **1958** — Frank Rosenblatt's perceptron: a single-layer learning machine, and the first AI winter's seed (its limits, shown by Minsky and Papert in 1969, froze neural-network funding for years).
- **1986** — Backpropagation popularized (Rumelhart, Hinton, Williams): multi-layer networks can actually learn, but compute is far too weak to use it at scale.
- **2012** — AlexNet wins ImageNet by a landslide using GPUs: the moment deep learning, big data, and graphics hardware meet. The GPU, built for games, becomes the AI machine — the origin of everything in §§1–7.
- **2017** — "Attention Is All You Need" (Vaswani et al.): the transformer replaces recurrence with self-attention, parallelizable across thousands of chips. Every frontier model since is a descendant.
- **2018–2020** — BERT, then GPT-3 (175 billion parameters): scale itself becomes the research program. Kaplan's scaling laws (2020) give the program its empirical footing.
- **2022** — Chinchilla corrects the allocation (smaller models, more data); ChatGPT launches November 30 and reaches mass adoption in weeks. AI stops being a research field and becomes a consumer product, then an arms race.
- **2023–2024** — GPT-4, open weights (LLaMA 2), multimodal models, agents. The first export controls (October 2022, tightened 2023) and the first copyright suits (NYT, December 2023) arrive within months of each other.
- **2025–2026** — Reasoning models, agents that use tools, and the buildout itself: gigawatt campuses, $100B+ annual capital expenditure per hyperscaler, HBM sold out through 2027, the first retirement wave, the first billion-dollar copyright settlement, and the EU AI Act's high-risk obligations taking effect. The history of AI is, in the end, a history of one idea — learning from data at scale — waiting seventy years for the hardware, the data, and the money to catch up with it, and then arriving all at once.

## 32. Recursive AI — the loop that feeds itself

"Recursive" gets used for three different things, at three different stages of real. Keeping them apart is the whole story.

**Level one: AI doing work.** The measure that matters comes from METR's March 2025 study of long tasks: the length of task a frontier agent can complete at 50% reliability has been **doubling roughly every 7 months since 2019**, faster in the 2024–25 window (about every 4 months). The o3-era horizon was ~110 minutes. The fine print matters: near-100% success on tasks taking a human under 4 minutes, under 10% on tasks over 4 hours — and the tasks are mostly coding, where goals are explicit, success is machine-checkable, and the sandbox resets cleanly. That is not the same as messy judgment work. Extrapolated, METR says month-long tasks within 5 years *if* the trend generalizes. That is an if, not a measurement.

**Level two: AI doing AI research.** The loop starts to close here:

- **AlphaEvolve** (Google DeepMind, May 2025): Gemini proposes algorithm variants, automated evaluators score them, winners seed the next round. Results: 4×4 complex matrix multiplication in **48 multiplications, beating Strassen's 49 from 1969**; a Borg scheduling heuristic recovering **0.7% of Google's fleet-wide compute**; a Gemini training kernel **23% faster, cutting total training time 1%**; matched the best known result on 75% of 50 open math problems and improved 20%.
- **The AI Scientist** (Sakana, 2024, v2 in 2025): the full research pipeline automated — ideas, experiments, analysis, figures, paper draft — for under $15 a paper, with one AI-generated paper reportedly passing human peer review at a workshop.
- **Absolute Zero Reasoner** (Tsinghua, NeurIPS 2025): a 7-billion-parameter coder proposing its own coding tasks and solving them, with **zero curated data**, gaining 10.2 points on combined math/code averages and 15.2 points on math from code-only self-play.

**Level three: AI rewriting itself.** The Darwin Gödel Machine (Sakana AI and the University of British Columbia, May 2025, ICLR 2026) is a coding agent that reads and modifies its own Python source, keeping an archive of variants and evolving lineages in parallel rather than hill-climbing from a single champion. SWE-bench went **20% → 50%**, Polyglot **14.2% → 30.7%**, over about 80 generations. The conceptual shift from Jürgen Schmidhuber's 2003 Gödel Machine is what made it practical: instead of demanding a *proof* of improvement before self-modification, it demands *empirical validation* on benchmarks. Sakana has since formalized the direction in a dedicated Recursive Self-Improvement Lab.

The labs' internal reports are bolder and should be read as self-reported: Anthropic's institute (June 2026) says Claude writes **80%+ of merged production code** and engineers ship ~8× the code per day versus 2024; OpenAI (September 2026) reports an "automated research intern" logging **3.1 agent-workdays per human workday**, with a stated goal of an automated AI researcher by March 2028. Internal, self-graded — not independent measurement.

**What is not real yet.** No system has run multiple generations of fully autonomous self-improvement. Humans still set the objective, build the evaluator, choose what to keep, and supply the compute at every step. The Darwin Gödel Machine improves a coding agent on benchmarks; it does not train its own successor. AlphaEvolve needs a human-defined score for each problem. The loop is real but narrow and human-gated — which is exactly why the classic frame needs updating rather than discarding. I.J. Good's 1965 "intelligence explosion" — an ultraintelligent machine designing even better machines, leaving human intelligence behind — assumed the loop could run away on its own. The 2025–2026 evidence says the loop runs, but through human-built gates: objectives, evaluators, benchmarks, and data centers.

**Why it belongs in this paper, two ways.** First, the loop runs on the paper's infrastructure: every generation needs fabs (§5), HBM (§4), power (§16), water (§18), and the balance sheet in §19 — so the infrastructure sections are both the accelerant and the brakes on any intelligence explosion. A loop that needs a gigawatt per generation cannot outrun the grid that feeds it. Second, it compounds §30's safety problem: a system that rewrites its own code gets harder to audit with each generation, and whether it stays steerable — corrigibility, the ability to shape and constrain a system even as it changes itself — becomes the central question. The scheming evidence in §30 was measured in systems that do not yet rewrite themselves; recursive systems add a moving target to an already hard oversight problem.

One distinction, kept explicit: this is a narrower, engineering version of the word than the recursivity in the author's framework — a loop feeding itself, not the same claim.

## 33. Children and the offloaded mind — what AI skips, and what never gets built

**The scale.** Pew Research Center (fall 2025): **54% of U.S. teens aged 13–17** use AI chatbots for schoolwork, and **10% do all or most of their work with chatbot help**. RAND's American Youth Panel: homework AI use rose **48% → 62%** between May and December 2025. In the UK, two-thirds of 13–18-year-olds used generative AI in 2025, with regular (weekly or more) use rising from 31.1% to 45.6%. The students see it themselves: **67% say AI harms critical thinking**, up from 54% earlier in 2025, and 42% of teens say they have used AI to do assignments *instead of* doing the work, up from 30% in 2024.

**The mechanism is cognitive offloading — and children are the sharp end of it.** Microsoft Research and Carnegie Mellon (CHI 2025; 319 knowledge workers, 936 tasks) found that the more people trusted the AI, the less critical thinking they put into checking it. The work shifts from producing to verifying — and verifying only works if you could still do the work yourself. An MIT Media Lab preprint (Kosmyna et al., 2025; EEG, 54 participants writing essays) found the ChatGPT group showed the weakest neural connectivity, and **83% could not quote a line from their own essay minutes later** — the researchers' term is "cognitive debt." The authors' own caveat stands: a small preprint on a single task, and lower brain activity is not proof of lasting damage. Oregon State researchers (Choudhuri, Sarma, et al.; 299 STEM students, five universities) found a "self-reinforcing cognitive debt cycle": routine use weakens thinking habits, which makes students lean on AI more, which weakens them further — with the surprise that the *most* tech-savvy students were the most prone, not the least. And the dependency signature: in a controlled study, students performed better *with* AI but worse than the control group when the tool was taken away. The skill was rented, not learned.

**The core argument: the answer was never the learning. The steps were.** Writing an essay is not about producing an essay. It is about forming a thesis, weighing evidence, structuring an argument, and holding a counterargument in mind long enough to answer it. Those steps are domain-general — the same steps used to evaluate a contract, a medical claim, a political argument, a business decision. When AI writes the essay, the student gets the product and skips every one of those steps. The same holds for mathematics: solving teaches decomposition, error-checking, and persistence through being stuck. The answer is the least important part. This is why "but they got the right answer" is a misleading metric: the transferable part of learning is the process, and offloading the process means there is nothing to carry into the next domain. The steps that would have translated elsewhere are never learned at all.

**The developmental asymmetry is what makes this a children's issue, not just a general AI issue.** An adult offloading has a foundation to fall back on — and still degrades, per the Microsoft/CMU study. A child offloading never builds the foundation in the first place. The steps have to be practiced while the brain is wiring up those pathways; skip them during that window and the result is not a degraded skill but an absent one. The difference between an adult using a calculator and a child never learning what multiplication *is*. Psychologist Lisanne Bainbridge made the general point in 1983, about the "ironies of automation": skills deteriorate when they are not used, and the generation that never did the work by hand cannot be expected to have those skills when the system fails. AI is the first automation of *thinking itself* rather than remembering or moving — which is why the generational version of her warning now applies to cognition directly.

**Replacement versus scaffold is the whole question.** Most teens use AI as tutor, not ghostwriter — research, math help, editing — and keen writers use it to enrich writing they already enjoy. Learning science has a name for what must be preserved: "desirable difficulties." Friction is where learning happens, and AI's whole design goal is removing friction. So the question is not AI yes or no; it is where the friction stays. "AI literacy" programs that only teach prompting miss the point entirely: prompting skill is not the missing piece. The missing piece is the struggle — the part where a student is stuck, working through it, building the step.

**The system is behind the students.** Only about a third of U.S. students say their school has a schoolwide AI policy; 64% of teens say their schools do not teach about AI as a learning tool; 68% of teachers report receiving no AI training at all. Meanwhile 55% of teens say doing homework with AI instead of doing it themselves is cheating — the norms are forming faster than the institutions. The policy gap matters because the default setting of every AI product is frictionless completion, and without deliberate counter-design — assessment that assumes AI is present, tasks that require showing the steps, friction placed where the learning lives — the default wins by itself.

## 34. Capstone — thermodynamics underneath it all: why efficiency doesn't save us

Every section of this paper is, at bottom, an energy story. This section names the principle underneath all of them.

**Jevons paradox, 1865.** William Stanley Jevons watched steam engines get more efficient at burning coal, and coal consumption went *up*, not down. Cheaper per unit of work means more work gets done, which means more total fuel. The pattern repeats in every efficiency chapter of this paper:

- Chips get more FLOPs per watt every generation — and total data center power goes 58 TWh (2014) → 192 TWh (2024) → 649 TWh projected for 2030 (§§8, 19). The efficiency did not save energy. It bought scale.
- Liquid cooling is more efficient than air — and it enables 120–150 kW racks (§7), so total heat goes up.
- The conversion tax (§16) gets shaved from ~25% loss toward ~2% — and the saved power becomes more racks.
- Heat reuse (§22) recovers energy — and the recovered energy justifies building more capacity next door.

Nobody ever announces the total. Each step is sold as a saving, and each saving is spent on more.

**Underneath that is the actual floor.** Landauer's principle: erasing one bit of information costs a minimum of energy, kT ln 2 — computation cannot be free, ever. The second law says every conversion loses something as heat. The conversion tax in §16 *is* thermodynamics: only 75–85% of grid power reaches the silicon, the rest becomes heat, and the heat in §21 is entropy's bill coming due. Efficiency cannot repeal the second law. It can only move the loss around — from the chip to the cooling tower to the river to the atmosphere to the climate. The industry operates nowhere near Landauer's floor, but the floor exists, and every number in this paper is a measurement of how far above it we operate. There is no version of this story in which the bill disappears. There are only versions in which it lands somewhere else.

**Why the trade-off stays invisible.** It is not hidden by conspiracy. It is hidden by speed and distribution:

- *Distributed costs, concentrated benefit.* The user gets the answer in seconds. The mine is in Congo (§20), the river is in Indonesia (§20), the grid strain is in Virginia (§21), the heat is over a neighborhood the user has never seen. The bill never arrives in one place, so no one feels its full weight.
- *Shifting baselines.* Each generation normalizes the new consumption level. 192 TWh sounds normal now because 58 TWh sounds like ancient history. The same normalization runs through §33: each cohort of students normalizes a new level of offloading.
- *Timescale mismatch.* The benefit is instant; the costs arrive over years — water tables, climate, attention, skill. Sub-threshold changes, baselines never recorded, so nobody notices until the total is enormous. The same slow pattern as the cognitive debt in §33 and the small differences in the author's synthesis work: change below the threshold of notice, accumulating until it is the new normal.

**The precise formulation.** Humans think efficiency means saving energy. Thermodynamics says efficiency means doing more per unit of energy — and a system that can do more, does more, until it hits the next constraint. The trade-off is not that efficiency fails. It is that efficiency succeeds, and success is spent on scale. The bill arrives diffused and delayed, and human attention is built to notice the fast thing (the answer, the saving, the capability) and miss the slow one (the heat, the water, the mine, the unwired skill).

Sections 1–33 are an inventory of where the bill lands. This section is why the bill always arrives: because underneath the racks, the markets, the models, and the children is thermodynamics — and thermodynamics does not negotiate.
